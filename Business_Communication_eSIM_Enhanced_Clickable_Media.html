Business communication is the exchange of information, ideas, instructions and feedback to achieve business and organisational objectives. It is essential when working in teams, writing emails, presenting ideas, resolving problems and interacting with customers.

Topic 1 · 5 minutes

Overview of Business Communication

1.1 What is business communication?

Business communication is the exchange of information, ideas, instructions and feedback between individuals or groups to achieve business and organisational objectives. It occurs internally between employees, managers and departments, and externally with customers, suppliers, partners and other stakeholders.

Internal communication

Example: A manager explains a new procedure to employees.

External communication

Example: A sales representative explains a product to a potential customer.

1.2 Why is it important?

  • Better teamwork: People understand roles, responsibilities and deadlines.
  • Improved productivity: Clear instructions reduce errors, duplicated work and delays.
  • Better decisions: Accurate information supports problem-solving.
  • Professional relationships: Respectful communication builds trust.
  • Persuasion and influence: Professionals present proposals and negotiate agreements.
  • Career development: Writing, speaking, listening and presentation skills demonstrate professionalism.

1.3 Characteristics of effective communication

Clear

The audience understands the meaning.

Accurate

Facts and details are correct.

Concise

Unnecessary words are removed.

Complete

Essential information is included.

Audience-centred

The message fits the receiver’s needs.

Professional

Tone, language and format are appropriate.

Workplace example

Vague: “Please finish the report soon.”

Better: “Please submit the completed report by Friday, 5.00 p.m. Include the findings, budget summary and recommendations.”

The improved instruction gives a clear deadline and explains what the report must contain.

Key takeaway: Send the right information to the right audience in a form they can understand and use.
Quick self-check: What makes an instruction effective?

An effective instruction is clear, accurate, concise and complete. It identifies the required action and, where relevant, the deadline and expected output.

Topic 2 · 10 minutes

Communication Process

2.1 The eight stages

The communication process explains how information moves from a sender to a receiver. Misunderstanding can occur at any stage.

1 · Sender has an ideaThe sender decides what needs to be communicated.
2 · EncodingThe sender converts the idea into words, symbols, images or gestures.
3 · Producing the messageThe sender prepares the email, presentation or other message.
4 · TransmittingThe sender selects a channel and sends the message.
5 · ReceivingThe receiver receives the email, instruction or presentation.
6 · DecodingThe receiver interprets the message and its meaning.
7 · RespondingThe receiver reacts, for example by asking a question or completing a task.
8 · FeedbackThe receiver responds so the sender can check understanding.

2.2 Communication in practice

Situation: A manager needs an employee to prepare a sales report.

StageExample
IdeaThe manager needs the latest sales figures.
Encoding and producingThe manager writes clear instructions in an email.
Transmitting and receivingThe email is sent and opened by the employee.
Decoding and respondingThe employee interprets the task and starts preparing the report.
FeedbackThe employee confirms the deadline and required format.

2.3 Communication channels

  • Face-to-face: Discussions, sensitive matters and immediate clarification.
  • Email: Formal messages, instructions and documented decisions.
  • Instant messaging: Short updates and quick coordination.
  • Video conferencing: Remote meetings and presentations.
  • Reports and presentations: Structured information, analysis and recommendations.

The best channel depends on purpose, urgency, complexity, confidentiality and audience.

Useful feedback

“Understood. I will submit the sales report by 3.00 p.m. tomorrow, including the monthly figures and a comparison with the previous month.”

This confirms the employee’s understanding of the task and deadline.

Key takeaway: Choose an appropriate channel and use feedback to confirm understanding.
Quick self-check: Is sending an email enough?

Not always. The receiver must receive and interpret the message, and feedback may be needed to confirm understanding. Sending a message does not guarantee successful communication.

Topic 3 · 15 minutes

Effective Communication Skills

Effective communication requires clear expression, careful listening, appropriate non-verbal behaviour and adaptation to the audience.

3.1 Verbal communication

Verbal communication uses spoken language to exchange information. Effective speakers speak clearly, organise ideas logically, use suitable language, avoid unnecessary jargon, maintain a professional tone and ask questions when clarification is needed.

Less effective: “The project is a mess.”
More effective: “The project is behind schedule because two deliverables remain incomplete. I recommend reallocating the tasks and reviewing the deadline.”

The improved statement focuses on facts and solutions rather than blame.

3.2 Written communication

Emails, reports, proposals, messages and formal letters should be clear, accurate, concise, complete and professional.

Before sending, check:
  1. Is the purpose clear?
  2. Is the information accurate?
  3. Is the tone appropriate?
  4. Are actions and deadlines stated?
  5. Have spelling, grammar and formatting been checked?
  6. Is the recipient correct?
Professional email example

Subject: Request for Project Meeting

Dear Team,

I would like to arrange a project meeting on Thursday at 10.00 a.m. to review our progress and discuss the remaining tasks. Please confirm your availability by Wednesday.

Thank you.
Best regards,
Project Coordinator

3.3 Active listening

Active listening means giving full attention to the speaker and deliberately trying to understand the message. Active listeners avoid interrupting, ask relevant questions, paraphrase important information and provide appropriate feedback.

Example

Colleague: “I am worried that we will not finish the presentation on time.”

Active response: “You are concerned about the deadline because several slides are incomplete. Which sections need the most support?”

3.4 Non-verbal communication

  • Facial expressions: May indicate interest, concern or confusion.
  • Gestures and posture: May communicate openness, confidence or discomfort.
  • Vocal characteristics: Tone, volume, pace and pauses influence interpretation.
  • Personal appearance: Can affect perceptions of professionalism.
  • Touching behaviour: Must respect professional boundaries and cultural expectations.
  • Time and space: Punctuality, distance and personal space can affect interactions.

Interpret non-verbal signals in context. A single gesture does not reliably reveal what someone thinks or feels.

3.5 Audience-centred communication

Adapt a message to the receiver’s knowledge, needs, expectations and circumstances. A technical manager may understand specialist terms, while a customer may need a simple explanation.

Ask yourself: Who is the audience? What do they know? What do they need? What action should they take? Which channel and tone are appropriate?

3.6 Constructive feedback

Constructive feedback focuses on observable work or outcomes and gives practical suggestions.

Less effective: “Your presentation is terrible.”
More effective: “Your presentation contains useful information, but the main recommendation is difficult to identify. Please highlight it on the first slide and simplify the supporting data.”
Key takeaway: Combine clear speaking and writing with active listening, suitable non-verbal behaviour and audience awareness.
Quick self-check: What is active listening?

Active listening means paying attention, checking understanding through questions or paraphrasing, and responding appropriately instead of merely waiting for your turn to speak.

Topic 4 · 10 minutes

Barriers to Communication

4.1 What are communication barriers?

Communication barriers are factors that prevent a message from being sent, received, understood or interpreted as intended. They may cause confusion, mistakes, delays, conflict and poor decisions.

4.2 Common barriers and solutions

1. Noise and physical distractions

Background noise, interruptions and poor audio can prevent people from receiving a message.

Solution: Choose a suitable environment, reduce distractions and summarise important information.

2. Language and unclear wording

Jargon, ambiguous instructions and language differences may cause misunderstanding.

Solution: Use plain language, explain unfamiliar terms and state specific dates or times.

3. Information overload

Too many emails, notifications and instructions may overwhelm the receiver.

Solution: Prioritise information, use meaningful subject lines and separate urgent messages from routine updates.

4. Emotional and psychological barriers

Stress, anger, anxiety, assumptions and defensiveness can affect interpretation.

Solution: Remain calm, focus on facts, ask for clarification and separate feedback about work from personal criticism.

5. Poor listening and selective perception

A receiver may focus only on part of the message or on information that confirms existing beliefs.

Solution: Listen actively, take notes, ask questions and confirm key instructions.

6. Cultural and interpersonal differences

People may have different expectations about formality, directness, gestures and feedback.

Solution: Use respectful language, avoid assumptions and clarify meaning when necessary.

7. Technology and channel problems

Poor internet connections, missing attachments, incorrect recipients and incompatible formats can interrupt communication.

Solution: Test technology, check attachments, use compatible formats and provide an alternative channel.

4.3 Five steps to overcome barriers

  1. Identify the source of the problem.
  2. Clarify the message or expectation.
  3. Select a more suitable channel if necessary.
  4. Confirm understanding through questions or feedback.
  5. Follow up to ensure the intended action occurs.
Workplace scenario

A supervisor sends: “Need the figures tomorrow. Send ASAP.” Team members do not know which figures are required, who is responsible or the exact deadline.

Improved message: “Please submit the March sales figures in the shared spreadsheet by 10.00 a.m. tomorrow. Team leaders are responsible for checking their department figures. Contact me if you need clarification.”

Key takeaway: Identify the source of misunderstanding and take practical steps to remove or reduce the barrier.
Quick self-check: Why is “ASAP” often ineffective?

It does not specify a precise deadline. A date and time make expectations clearer and easier to follow.

Topic 5 · 10 minutes

Digital Communication Tools

5.1 What are digital communication tools?

Digital communication tools are technologies that allow people to exchange information, collaborate and work together electronically with colleagues, customers, suppliers and business partners.

5.2 Common tools and appropriate uses

Email

Best for: Formal requests, instructions, reports and written records.

Professional practice: Use a meaningful subject line, state the purpose, check attachments and recipients, and avoid unnecessary “Reply All”.

Instant messaging

Examples: Microsoft Teams chat and WhatsApp.

Best for: Short updates and quick clarification.

Professional practice: Keep messages concise, respect working hours, avoid unnecessary repeated messages and do not assume a message has been understood.

Video conferencing

Examples: Microsoft Teams, Zoom and Google Meet.

Best for: Virtual meetings, presentations and remote discussions.

Professional practice: Check audio and connectivity, join punctually, mute when not speaking, encourage participation and summarise decisions.

Collaborative documents and cloud platforms

Examples: Google Docs and Microsoft 365.

Best for: Joint report writing, document review and sharing files.

Professional practice: Use clear file names, manage access permissions, track revisions and protect confidential information.

Project management platforms

Examples: Trello, Asana and Microsoft Planner.

Best for: Assigning tasks, tracking progress and managing deadlines.

Professional practice: Assign task owners, set deadlines, update task status and record important decisions.

5.3 Choose the right channel

Workplace needSuitable channel
Formal request or documented instructionEmail
Quick clarificationInstant messaging
Sensitive or complex discussionFace-to-face or video meeting
Joint report preparationCollaborative document
Tracking tasks and deadlinesProject management platform
Presenting findings to a groupVideo conference or presentation

The best choice depends on urgency, complexity, confidentiality, audience preference and organisational policy.

5.4 Digital communication etiquette

  • Use professional language, standard spelling and grammar.
  • Respect privacy and confidentiality.
  • Check information before forwarding it.
  • Avoid personal attacks and emotionally charged replies.
  • Protect accounts with strong passwords and appropriate security measures.
  • Verify recipients and access permissions.
  • Avoid unnecessary notifications and messages.
  • Never assume online communication is automatically private.
Workplace scenario: A project team working remotely
  • Teams chat: Quick updates and clarification.
  • Shared PowerPoint: Collaborative slide preparation.
  • Planner: Task allocation and deadline tracking.
  • Video meeting: Review slides and resolve complex issues.
  • Email: Send the final approved presentation to the lecturer or client.
The team should agree on communication rules, file locations, responsibilities and deadlines at the start.
Key takeaway: Select technology according to the task, maintain professional etiquette and protect information.
Quick self-check: Which tool should a team use to track task ownership and deadlines?

A project management platform such as Microsoft Planner, Trello or Asana is suitable because it can display tasks, responsible persons, status and deadlines.
